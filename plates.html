Event log

    Using the page

    Views. Drag the globe to turn it; on the map, drag to pan. Scroll or pinch to zoom, on both and on the L1 map. A tap or click without dragging selects that point: its landmass is highlighted, its rock column and fault history appear under the map, and the L1 panel shows the landmass as L0 has it now.

    Running. Run advances L0 by the Gyr slider and stops; Run again continues for that long. Step advances one step. Marks shows hotspots (yellow rings) and plate velocity arrows. Net rotation of the lithosphere is always removed (see below).

    Map projection. Interrupted Goode homolosine, an equal-area projection: sinusoidal within 40°44′ of the equator and Mollweide beyond. Its cuts are placed for this planet: the map edge is the meridian crossing the least land, the north gets one more cut and the south two, each at the meridian crossing the least land in that hemisphere, at least 50° from the others. Cuts are re-placed when the world stops moving.

    Legends. Elevation: sea level is the blue–green break; dark lines are trenches. Rock type: darker means older. L1 preview: smoothed L0 elevation and formations, cut by stand-in fine relief (noise, not a real L1 model). Plates: light fill is continental crust; boundaries coloured by relative motion.

    L1 landmass. Refine selected landmass replays the last 10–30 Myr of the selected landmass’s history from a saved state of L0, at finer resolution and with the continents deforming instead of rigid. Its crust is a thin viscous sheet: each plate drags the crust above it, and thickened crust pushes back by its own weight, so where plates converge the crust crumples into mountain belts and plateaus. The sheet carries L0’s rock columns. At the end, tectonics stops and 5 Myr of erosion on a 10 km grid settle the drainage basins, the tiles for L2. L0 ends exactly where it started. Buffer sets which land counts as the landmass: islands within that distance of its coast join it (and islands within that distance of those), and seas it encloses are always part of it, so an archipelago or an inland sea is refined with the land around it rather than cut off at a straight edge. Before refining, the L1 map shows L0’s present surface around the landmass (the landmass in colour, other land grey); refinement starts from L0’s state one window earlier.

    L1 views. Terrain: shaded relief; outside the landmass’s crust, plain ocean (not modelled at L1; before refining, the preview shows L0’s surface there, dimmed). Thickening: crust thickened (red) or thinned (blue) since the window began. Finite strain: total horizontal shortening (red) and extension (blue) where it has not shortened; ticks lie along the shortening direction where it exceeds 10%. Strain rate: how fast the sheet deforms now (log scale). Surface rock: topmost unit by volume. Mantle pull: dynamic topography from sinking slabs, included in the elevation. Rainfall: a crude climate of latitude belts, zonal winds and rain shadows. Drainage basins: one colour per basin of at least 10,000 km²; inland-draining basins in earth tones, areic desert sand-coloured, small coastal basins grey; lakes blue (overflowing), pale blue (no outlet), white (dry floor). L2 tiles: the tiles L1 hands to L2, dark edges where the divide is firm (re-seeding the stand-in fine relief, 77% of firm divides stay within 30 km, against about half of the others).

    L2 basins. Once a landmass is refined, a tap on its map (in any view but L2 tiles) selects drainage basins for L2: the tapped basin, whole, whatever its size; every basin lying wholly within the selection radius of the tap; and every basin the selection encloses. The circle shows the radius; land outside the selection is faded. A tap at sea selects only the basins wholly within the radius.

    L2 relief. Preview L2 relief draws the selected basins (land outside them faded) as L2 will start from them: L1’s surface, plus relief at 1–16 km that L1 cannot resolve (as loud as L1’s own local roughness continued down the spectrum, stretched along strike where L1 shortened the crust), with L1’s large rivers kept open in narrow valleys. It is a function of position, so each zoom level is drawn at its own pixel size; scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan, tap for the elevation.

    Run L2. Evolves the selected basins at 500 m for the run length at present rates: L1’s uplift, L1’s rainfall weighting the water, erodibility from the rock at the surface (sediments weak, cratonic basement strong), and rivers cutting by stream power. Hollows the run’s sediment can fill become flats; the outline is a fixed divide; afterwards the change averaged over each 10 km cell is removed, so L2 alters only what L1 can’t resolve. Views: terrain (rivers draining 5 km² and more in blue), change (incision blue, fill red), drainage area, and stream order (Strahler).

    L1 handoff. Extract handoff freezes the world and packages what L1 needs: a rock column for every cell, a fault network traced from the slip each crust parcel has recorded, the event catalogue and current plate motions.

    Export session. Saves a small JSON log from which the session reruns exactly: seed, settings and step counts; the selected point; the L1 refinement’s cell, saved state, window and settings; check values for the rerun; what each view shows and how it is zoomed and coloured (L0, the L1 map and the L2 tiles); the statistics, the event log and the actions taken. tools/session.js in the repository reruns it.

    Core. The lattice core stores each plate on its own grid and resamples the plates onto the fixed grid every step. The marker core (new; Stage 1 of the plan in L0_MESH_SPEC.md) carries the crust on markers that move with their plates, on a triangulated mesh, so nothing is resampled; it keeps the lattice core’s rules, starts from the same world, and is about four times slower. It runs in a background worker, so the page stays responsive while it does; the inspector’s column and the handoff take a moment to arrive. Markers draws them: faint dots inside plates, coloured dots on boundaries (ridge, trench or collision, transform; white at triple junctions). L1 refinement still needs the lattice core.

    What’s modeled, and what’s faked

    Grid. A cube-sphere: six faces of n × n cells projected onto an Earth-sized sphere, with constant-time lookup of the cell under any direction. Cell sizes vary by about ±25% across a face.

    Plates. Each plate stores its crust in its own body frame and rotates rigidly about an Euler pole, with angular velocity drifting randomly. Each plate’s speed relaxes toward its own preferred value (a free 3D random walk would creep outward until it hit the 8 cm/yr cap). Every step, each fixed cell asks which plates now cover it.

    Net rotation. Independent random plate motions add up to a shared spin of the whole lithosphere over the mantle, which grows over time, like the flying ice cube in molecular dynamics. Every step computes that shared spin (each cell’s velocity weighted by its area, minus the part along its own position) and subtracts it from every plate. Relative motions, and so all boundaries and faults, are unchanged; only hotspot tracks and continents’ latitudes are affected. Earth’s lithosphere does drift slowly relative to hotspots, roughly 0.1–0.4° per million years by different estimates, so keeping some would not be wrong, just unconstrained; the page always removes it.

    Boundaries. Where two plates overlap, denser crust is consumed: oceanic under continental, older oceanic under younger. Subduction thickens the overriding plate 1–3 cells inland and marks a trench. Continent–continent collision keeps all of the consumed crust: head-on, it underthrusts and thickens the winner a few cells inland; sideways, it piles up at the boundary. Continental slices pushed onto seafloor accrete as continent. Sustained collision welds plates. Uncovered cells get new seafloor of age zero, which is how ridges form.

    Rock columns. Every crust parcel carries a stack of up to eight units, bottom to top, each with a rock type, formation time and thickness. Deposition pushes units: arc volcanics, hotspot or flood basalt, collisional metamorphics, marine shelf sediments, and clastic sediments shed by erosion. Erosion shaves the top and pops units, exposing older rock. Thickening at depth goes into the basement unit. Each unit remembers the plate it formed on. Same-type units from the same plate formed within 150 million years merge, and the two deepest fold together when the stack is full. Eroded sediment is spread over every lower neighbour in proportion to its drop. The Rock type map shows the rock with the most thickness in the top kilometre, not the topmost veneer.

    Faults. At every plate-boundary cell, the relative motion is split into an across-boundary part (convergence or extension) and an along-boundary part. Those slips accumulate in the parcel’s fault record, along with the boundary’s strike, stored in the plate’s own frame so dead faults ride along with the crust. A record keeps the current and previous episode; a new one starts after 10 million years of quiet or of a different style. Continental collisions now thicken crust in proportion to the across-boundary share of motion, so oblique collisions build less relief.

    Formations and domains. At handoff, units in neighbouring continental columns are linked when their rock type and origin plate match and their ages agree (within 40 million years, or 10% of their age for old rock), walking both stacks bottom to top so order is preserved. Linked units become formations. Neighbours on the same plate whose stacks share at least 40% of their thickness by matched units form a domain; slivers under eight columns join a neighbouring domain.

    L1 preview. Elevation is smoothed over about three cells and interpolated bilinearly on the cube grid; the coast is its zero contour. Formation thicknesses are averaged over neighbours in the same domain (absent counts as zero, so isolated specks fade and edges pinch out) and interpolated per pixel without crossing domain boundaries. A ridged noise field stands in for L1 relief: valleys cut deeper into the formation stack and expose older units. The noise is a placeholder for a real L1 terrain model.

    L1 landmass. L0 saves its whole state every 10 Myr (the last four). Picking a landmass at the present restores the newest state older than the window and replays to the present, tagging crust parcels: the landmass’s crust is every parcel that ends up in it, plus continental crust that touches it during the window and is gone by the end (consumed in a collision). L0 is deterministic, so the replay lands exactly on the present. A second replay runs the sheet alongside, in an azimuthal equidistant map co-moving with the plate that carries most of the landmass. Markers (36 per L0 cell) carry each parcel’s crust thickness, rock column and strength (old cratons ten times stronger). Each step the sheet’s velocity solves the thin viscous sheet equations (England and McKenzie 1982): power-law viscosity with exponent 3, buoyancy from the crust’s excess potential energy, and a basal drag toward the velocity of the plate L0 says carries each marker. Free edges, conjugate gradients on a staggered grid, two Picard iterations. The Argand number sets the viscosity at a reference strain rate of 50 km/Myr over the coupling length. Markers move with the sheet and thicken by its convergence, conserving volume exactly. L0’s arcs and hotspots add crust under the markers, a new top unit of arc rock. Elevation is the inherited elevation plus the thickness change minus a flexural root (40 km flexural length, the plate yielding beyond 3 km from local balance). Rivers on the sheet grid erode by the stream-power law; eroded rock leaves the system. A volume budget closes to rounding error. The drainage pass then refines the final surface to 10 km (bilinear plus a little fixed noise), runs 5 Myr of stream-power erosion with tectonics frozen, and labels every land cell by the outlet it drains to.

    Handoff. Extraction groups continental cells with at least 60 km of recorded slip and similar strike, then traces each group into polylines. Its kind is thrust, normal or strike-slip from the summed slip. Rock columns and faults are copied when crust is copied, and orphaned records are garbage-collected.

    Elevation. Derived, not stored: Airy isostasy for continents, square-root-of-age cooling for seafloor. Hotspots are fixed in the mantle frame, so moving plates drag curved island chains off them.

    Surface and budget. Continental crust diffuses and erodes; sediment builds shelves. A feedback term speeds margin growth when continental area is below the chosen target and makes drowned margins founder back to seafloor when it’s above. The Continental crust slider sets both the starting fraction and that target. Land above sea is roughly half of it in the long run, because erosion wears continents toward sea level; Earth has about 40% continental crust (including shelves) and 29% land. This thermostat is a deliberate fudge to keep the crust budget stable, not physics.

    Stale cells. Plate-grid cells that no fixed cell samples in a step miss that step’s surface processes. Each step, such a cell surrounded by sampled neighbours takes their crust type and mean thickness, so old seafloor can’t hide inside a continent that grew around it. When plates weld, the surviving plate’s grid pulls crust from the absorbed plate for every cell on the absorbed plate’s territory, rather than each absorbed cell pushing to its nearest target, which left holes.

    Known artifacts. Blocky streaks in seafloor age come from nearest-cell resampling as plates rotate. The same resampling makes ragged boundaries, so many continental margins carry some fault record, and extracted traces are fragmented into segments a few hundred kilometres long. Most remaining flicker in the Rock type map comes from that resampling: about one cell in five samples a different crust parcel each step. The handoff smooths it out, but L0 itself would need a finer plate grid or moving particles to remove it. The same seed and settings always reproduce the same world.
